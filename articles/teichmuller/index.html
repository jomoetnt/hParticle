Inter-Universal Teichmüller Theory

The German mathematician Oswald Teichmüller was born in 1913 and quickly distinguished himself as a genius. He was a staunch supporter of the Nazi party as early as 1931, joining its military wing, and organizing boycotts against his Jewish professors. After becoming a professor at the University of Göttingen in 1935, publishing good work, he was drafted in 1939 to fight in World War II. Two years later, Teichmüller was released from the military at the request of fellow Nazi mathematician Ludwig Bieberbach so that he could continue teaching. However, Teichmüller voluntarily left his position soon after to fight on the Eastern Front after hearing about the German defeat at Stalingrad, and mysteriously disappeared following the destruction of his unit. He left us with the concept of a Teichmüller space, the study of which is called Teichmüller theory. Several generalizations of this theory have been studied over the years, but the topic of this article will be its most recent and humorous form.

Shinichi Mochizuki and the abc Conjecture

The abc conjecture is one of the most important unsolved problems in number theory. However, not everyone agrees with the "unsolved" part of that sentence. In 2012, a Japanese mathematician named Shinichi Mochizuki unleashed Inter-Universal Teichmüller theory (IUTT) onto the world. His papers spanned 500 dense pages and contained the most esoteric, incomprehensible mathematics that has likely ever been produced by a professional researcher. In these papers, he claims to solve several famous problems in arithmetic geometry, perhaps most notably the abc conjecture. This immediately caused a stir in the mathematical community, but even serious mathematicians were unable to understand his proofs due to the sheer complexity of IUTT. For years, these works remained unpublished because no journal was able to verify the theory, until 2021, when the Publications of the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences finally did so. There remained one small problem however; not only was Mochizuki a professor at the Research Institute for Mathematical Sciences, he was actually the editor-in-chief of its journal.
There are truly only a handful of people worldwide that have the necessary prerequisites to even begin studying IUTT, one of which being Mochizuki's theory of mono-abelian geometry, which, unlike IUTT, is actually well-accepted.
Needless to say, the greater mathematical community remains unconvinced that the abc conjecture is actually proven. A few brave souls who decided to sink their teeth into IUTT shortly after its debut expressed their confusion about a section in Mochizuki's third paper, namely Corollary 3.12. One of the world's leading mathematicians, Peter Scholze, visited Mochizuki in 2018 to discuss issues with IUTT such as this for five days, though neither were convinced by each other. Scholze released a report along with his colleague Jakob Stix where they stated that Corollary 3.12 has an error that cannot be easily repaired, rendering the proof of the abc conjecture invalid. Mochizuki responded by releasing his own report saying that they oversimplified IUTT, maintaining that his proof is correct. The publication of his papers in 2021 proceeded despite this dispute and Scholze has said that the issues he previously outlined are still present in the final version. Recent attempts to verify IUTT using computers have shown that the argument used to arrive at Corollary 3.12 is unformalizable, which just so happens to be the exact step which Scholze claims has a gap in logic.
Contrary to what you might expect in a field as precise and rigorous as mathematics, several established researchers still believe that IUTT has resolved the abc conjecture. Primarily in Japan, and especially at Mochziuki's home institution of Kyoto University, certain mathematicians reject the flaws identified by Scholze and company, instead choosing to accept the theory as-is. To add even more chaos into the mix, Professor Kirti Joshi of the University of Arizona claims that Scholze's camp indeed misunderstood IUTT, and that it has true mathematical value, but that certain portions are incorrect as originally proposed. Joshi put forward numerous papers examining IUTT in more detail and, according to him, fixing it to some extent. However, despite having something of an ally outside of Japan for once (aside from Ivan Fesenko), Mochizuki proceeded to release a scathing report in response calling Joshi "profoundly ignorant" and saying that his papers "[do] not contain... any meaningful mathematical content whatsoever" (the report is pretty funny to read if you have a minute). As of yet, Joshi's work on IUTT has not been met with great interest from other mathematicians, even Scholze (and Mochizuki, of course).
The timeline of events, as summarized by Joshi (and further summarized by myself), is Mochizuki releasing his IUTT papers, Scholze critiquing them, Joshi fixing them, and then Mochizuki responding in LowTierGod fashion.

Conclusion

The future of IUTT is uncertain at this point. Most mathematicians refuse to pay attention to it on account of Mochizuki's failure to clearly convey his ideas (and his refusal to engage with skeptics). Even if the theory is correct, a common criticism of IUTT is its lack of mathematical value outside of supposedly solving a few notable problems in number theory; there are no additional questions that naturally follow, and the growth of mathematics as a subject requires more than just proving theorems. If by some miracle the efforts to formally verify IUTT pan out, we may see some mainstream interest in it, although there are very few people worldwide who would be able to contribute to its further developments. Though it would be entertaining to see the fallout from such an event, in all likelihood, IUTT will eventually be relegated to obscurity, or perhaps taught as a cautionary tale to future students on how not to go about doing professional mathematics.
